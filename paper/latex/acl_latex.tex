\begin{abstract}
Multimodal LLMs are often evaluated on medical image questions using final-answer accuracy, which does not reveal whether answers depend on the image, the text, or shortcuts. We build five-phase question chains from the LUMIERE glioblastoma cohort and first audit an expert-labeled version. A slice-based enhancement rule reproduces the expert RANO rating for \vfourConcordantPct{}\% of follow-ups (\vfourConcordantDetPct{}\% when determinate), so we do not treat those ratings as validated targets for the displayed slices.

We rebuild the benchmark with explicit key provenance: rendered sequence (AIA), segmentation-derived lesion position (LIL) and RANO category (DSCR), and a rule-based management decision using response category and time since chemoradiotherapy (TCM). We evaluate four open-weight models and one reference model using image-only controls, matched image swaps, fixed-context comparisons, and management-rule fact flips, reporting accuracy benefit, output sensitivity, and donor tracking separately.

The reference model extracts sequence and lesion side from the image (\vfourEoneOwnAIAGemini{}\% and \vfourEoneOwnLILGemini{}\%, versus \vfourEoneTextAIAGemini{}\% and \vfourEoneTextLILGemini{}\% without it) and tracks swapped images. Open models show limited and inconsistent image-dependent performance: sequence accuracy ranges from \vfourEoneOwnAIAGemmaTwelve{}\% to \vfourEoneOwnAIAScout{}\% (25\% chance), only one image-effect test survives multiplicity correction (Llama-4-Scout on the diagnosis category, where its accuracy with the image stays below the share of the most common key and its text-only accuracy is far below it), and donor tracking with an interval above zero appears only for MedGemma-4B on sequence and Llama-4-Scout on the diagnosis category. No model exceeds the best category lookup on the management rule, and stating the rule in the prompt raises accuracy for three of the four (to \vfourExplAccScout{}--\vfourExplAccGemmaTwelve{}\%) and leaves MedGemma-4B unchanged. We therefore characterize observable image-dependent behavior, not internal image use or learned shortcuts. LIL, DSCR, and TCM keys are segmentation- or rule-derived and are not clinician-validated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Medical multimodal LLMs are often evaluated by final-answer accuracy, which cannot distinguish image-based reasoning from reliance on textual cues, option patterns, or guessing. Such shortcuts are well documented: models exploit annotation artifacts in natural language inference \citep{gururangan2018annotation}, syntactic heuristics \citep{mccoy2019right}, and unfaithful stated reasoning \citep{turpin2023language}. In chest-radiograph question answering, models can rely on linguistic patterns or irrelevant image regions \citep{nguyen2025localizing}. Image ablation or swapping is informative only when the displayed image contains enough evidence to recover the answer.

We ask whether accuracy on brain-MRI questions reflects use of the scan or a shortcut, treating answerability as something to audit rather than assume. Using the five OmniBrainBench phases \citep{peng2026omnibrainbench}, we construct same-patient question chains from the LUMIERE glioblastoma cohort \citep{suter2022lumiere}: anatomical and imaging assessment (AIA), lesion identification and localization (LIL), diagnostic synthesis and causal reasoning (DSCR), prognostic judgment and risk forecasting (PJRF), and therapeutic cycle management (TCM). An audit of the expert RANO ratings showed that they cannot be treated as validated targets for the displayed slices (Section~\ref{sec:audit}), which motivates a benchmark with explicit key provenance and fixed controls (Sections~\ref{sec:v4}--\ref{sec:controls}).

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item An automated reproducibility audit showing that an enhancement-based rule reproduces the expert RANO category for \vfourConcordantPct{}\% of \vfourFollowups{} follow-ups (\vfourConcordantDetPct{}\% of the \vfourDeterminate{} determinate cases). We therefore do not treat those ratings as validated targets for the displayed slices. We also find only 2 verifiable cross-phase links among 6,823 released questions, so same-patient continuity is not established by the available identifiers and matching procedure.
    \item A rebuilt chain with explicit key provenance by phase and a prespecified control suite comprising image-only positive controls, matched donor-image swaps, fixed-context comparisons, and fact-flip tests of the management rule.
    \item An evaluation of four open-weight models and one reference model that separates accuracy benefit, output sensitivity, and donor tracking. The reference model shows image-dependent performance on sequence and lesion-side questions, whereas the open models are limited and inconsistent; on the diagnosis-category task the most common key covers \vfourMajorityDSCR{}\% of items, so accuracy there is hard to separate from a class prior even for the reference model.
\end{itemize}




\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{Shortcuts and faithfulness.} Models can reach correct labels without performing the intended reasoning. Hypothesis-only baselines exploit annotation artifacts in NLI datasets \citep{poliak2018hypothesis,gururangan2018annotation}, models adopt heuristics that fail on HANS \citep{mccoy2019right}, and biased input features can alter chain-of-thought answers without appearing in the stated rationale \citep{turpin2023language}.

\paragraph{Answering without the image.} Visual question-answering models often exploit language priors. \citet{goyal2017making} countered this by pairing questions with images yielding different answers, while \citet{chen2024right} show that many multimodal benchmark items do not require visual content. Similar failures appear in medicine: \citet{yan2025worse} report severe degradation under adversarial diagnostic counterparts, \citet{jin2024hidden} find flawed rationales in 35.5\% of GPT-4V's correct answers, and \citet{nguyen2025localizing} use textual and visual perturbations to localize shortcuts in chest-radiograph QA. We extend this logic to longitudinal brain-MRI chains using image-only controls, matched image swaps, and interventions on supplied clinical facts. UniMod \citep{gu2026unimod} addresses modality imbalance during training by supervising image-only, text-only, and fused predictions separately; we instead evaluate existing models for observable image dependence.

\paragraph{Brain benchmarks and longitudinal data.} OmniBrainBench \citep{peng2026omnibrainbench} contains 6,823 closed-ended brain-imaging questions spanning five clinical phases and 15 modalities. Its supplement describes a patient-level ADNI extension, but we did not evaluate it. LUMIERE \citep{suter2022lumiere} and MU-Glioma-Post \citep{muglioma2025post} provide longitudinal glioma imaging, treatment, and outcome data but no question layer.





\section{Building Chains from LUMIERE}
\label{sec:lumiere}

\paragraph{Why not OmniBrainBench.} A chain needs one patient's phases. We checked whether any image is shared across questions of different phases within a grouped case: of 6,823 closed-ended questions only 2 verifiable cross-phase patient links exist (both in VQA\_RAD, one AIA and one LIL question each), and a second method based on filename identifiers agreed; the counts are lower bounds (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-compat}). Each sub-corpus was built for one task, so a patient is essentially asked one phase-type of question. This is an incompatibility between the released groupings and our evaluation, not a defect in a promise its authors made.

\paragraph{LUMIERE.} LUMIERE \citep{suter2022lumiere} has 91 glioblastoma patients with longitudinal MRI (T1, contrast-enhanced T1, T2, FLAIR), automated segmentations (DeepBraTumIA and HD-GLIO-AUTO; \citealp{suter2022lumiere, kickingereder2019automated}), RANO ratings by one neuroradiologist \citep{wen2010updated}, and overall survival for most patients. Segmentations are automated and were not manually verified; the ratings are expert-assigned.

\subsection{Audit: can an automated rule reproduce the expert ratings from the displayed slices?}
\label{sec:audit}

The natural key for a diagnosis-category question is the expert RANO rating, but whether that rating can be recovered from the displayed slices is untested. We tested it with an automated reproducibility audit: for each first-line follow-up we tracked one enhancing lesion across scans (at each scan, the 3D-connected enhancing component nearest to the largest component of the post-operative baseline), measured it on its own largest axial cross-section, computed the RANO bidimensional product (longest diameter times longest perpendicular diameter) and applied the enhancement criteria (progression: measurable disease at least 25\% above the smallest earlier measurement, the nadir, or measurable disease where the nadir had none; response against the post-operative baseline; measurable means both diameters at least 10 mm). The rule has no new-lesion criterion beyond that. Of \vfourFollowups{} first-line follow-ups (one per rated, imaged timepoint before any second surgery), \vfourUndetermined{} are undetermined because no measurable enhancing disease exists on the baseline or nadir scan. Of the \vfourDeterminate{} determinate follow-ups the rule reproduces the expert category for \vfourConcordant{} (\vfourConcordantDetPct{}\%, Wilson 95\% interval \vfourConcordantDetLo{} to \vfourConcordantDetHi{}\%; \vfourConcordantPct{}\% if the undetermined ones are counted as failures): progressive disease \vfourExpPDSame{} of \vfourExpPDN{}, complete response \vfourExpCRSame{} of \vfourExpCRN{}, stable disease \vfourExpSDSame{} of \vfourExpSDN{}, partial response \vfourExpPRSame{} of \vfourExpPRN{} (these denominators include the undetermined ones). Of the \vfourDisagree{} disagreements, the rule returns complete response for \vfourDisRuleCR{} (the expert rated progressive disease for \vfourConfPDasCR{} and stable disease for \vfourConfSDasCR{}) and progressive disease for \vfourDisRulePD{} (\vfourConfSDasPD{} rated stable by the expert). Disagreement can arise from errors in the automated segmentation or in our slice choice and measurement, from a different target lesion or reference scan than the rater's, from differences between our simplified rule and the rater's assessment (which may use T2/FLAIR change, clinical status and steroid dose), or from evidence absent from the displayed slices; this audit does not separate them. LUMIERE documents that one neuroradiologist rated the studies but not what information they used. Our automated product correlates only moderately with the diameters the rater recorded for the target lesion (Spearman \vfourBpRho{} over \vfourBpN{} follow-ups; ours is a median \vfourBpRatio{} times larger), so measurement error is one plausible contributor. Where the rater recorded target-lesion diameters (\vfourSizeN{} follow-ups, \vfourSizeExpertPD{} of them rated progressive disease), agreement was the same whether our product lay within a factor of 1.5 of the rater's (\vfourSizeCloseAgree{} of \vfourSizeCloseN{}) or not (\vfourSizeOtherAgree{} of \vfourSizeOtherN{}), so a size discrepancy does not explain the disagreements in that subset; it holds few stable-disease or partial-response ratings, where the disagreement is concentrated, so it cannot test them. The audit therefore shows that these ratings cannot be treated as validated targets for the displayed inputs; it does not measure how many of them are unanswerable, which would need qualified reviewers to assess the displayed inputs for a sample of the disagreements.

\subsection{The item set}
\label{sec:v4}

The keys have four different origins (Table~\ref{tab:phases}), and we make no uniform claim that each is a function of what the model sees. AIA: the sequence rendered (acquisition-derived). LIL and DSCR: derived from automated segmentation masks that the model does not see, so a reader can recover them from the slices only to the extent that the segmentation is right and the displayed slice shows it; they are segmentation-derived targets that reviewers have not yet validated. PJRF: the recorded outcome (death within 52 weeks), scored probabilistically, since a future outcome is not a function of the facts stated. TCM: the output of an explicitly stated management rule applied to the category and timing given in the stem, which tests following a stipulated rule, not clinical correctness. All random choices use fixed seeds and never look at model output.

\paragraph{Cohort.} Of the patients with a first-line follow-up (rated, imaged, before any second surgery, with an imaged post-operative reference scan), we select one follow-up per patient in strata defined by the TCM rule class (continue, repeat MRI first, change therapy) and by the DSCR category, with quotas fixed in advance and a fixed seed, giving \vfourPatients{} patients. Selection used tabular records and segmentation masks only. Progressive-disease follow-ups whose timing falls within 2 weeks of the 12-week window are left out.

\paragraph{Images.} Each image is an atlas-space axial slice, skull-stripped by the dataset authors with HD-BET \citep{isensee2019automated}, at 4$\times$ magnification with a 20\,mm scale bar, in radiological orientation (checked against the affine). Slice levels are those on which the key is computed.

\paragraph{AIA, LIL, DSCR.} AIA is the positive control: its key is the sequence rendered, balanced over four sequences (the most common covers \vfourMajorityAIA{}\% of items), so text alone can reach only chance in expectation. LIL's key is the hemisphere and anterior/posterior half of the largest tumor-core component on the displayed slice; lesions within 10\,mm of a midline, smaller than 100\,mm$^2$ or with under 80\% of their pixels on their centroid's side of the midline are not asked about. DSCR's stem states the RANO enhancement criteria, and its key is the category computed from the bidimensional products. The expert rating is recorded for each item but is not the key.

\paragraph{PJRF.} The prediction time is the follow-up scan, the available information is what the stem states plus the model's earlier assessment, and the outcome is death within 52 weeks of the scan, from the LUMIERE survival record (which we assume shares its origin with the week numbering; LUMIERE states that all dates are relative to the pre-operative image, but neither its paper nor its readme states the origin of overall survival or mentions censoring; \vfourPjrfOsShort{} of \vfourPjrfOsN{} patients with a survival time have one shorter than their last imaging week, \vfourPjrfOsShortCohort{} of them in our cohort, and \vfourPjrfFlipFour{} of the \vfourNPJRF{} outcome labels would change under a four-week shift of the origin). The model picks one of four probability bins (midpoints .10, .30, .50, .80) and is scored with the Brier score, not by whether it picked a ``correct'' bin. The recorded outcome is death in \vfourPjrfBase{}\% of \vfourPjrfN{} patients; a leave-one-out logistic model on the stated facts reaches Brier \vfourPjrfBrierLR{} against \vfourPjrfBrierBase{} for the constant base rate (AUC \vfourPjrfAucLR{}); in this small sample that model did not improve on the base rate, which does not show that the facts carry no predictive information. The base rate reflects our selection by response and management class and is not a population rate, and because the time origin and censoring are not verified, PJRF is a secondary analysis and no other claim in this paper rests on it.

\paragraph{TCM.} LUMIERE records no treatment after chemoradiotherapy, so a documented treatment is not available and the key is a rule-based recommendation, not what was done: non-progressive disease continues adjuvant temozolomide; progressive disease within 12 weeks of completing radiotherapy is confirmed with repeat MRI before any change; later progressive disease prompts a change of therapy. The 12-week window is from the RANO criteria \citep{wen2010updated}, which also allow progression inside the window when new enhancement lies outside the radiation field; we cannot assess the field, so the rule is a simplification. Radiotherapy dates are not in LUMIERE, so the stem states the weeks since chemoradiotherapy under an assumed standard schedule (ending 10 weeks after surgery). The same diagnosis therefore maps to different actions depending on the stated timing (\vfourTcmConfirm{} repeat-MRI and \vfourTcmEscalate{} change-therapy items among \vfourTcmPdItems{} progressive-disease items), which lets a lookup of the category alone be told apart from use of the timing fact.




\section{Controls and Pre-specified Analysis}
\label{sec:controls}

The analysis plan (hypotheses, margin, correction family and decision rules) was written before any model run; its hash is recorded in the project log, and the file is not externally timestamped. Models are the four open-weight models (MedGemma-4B \citep{sellergren2025medgemma}, Gemma-3-12B and Gemma-3-27B \citep{gemmateam2025gemma3}, Llama-4-Scout; 4-bit, greedy decoding, 800 tokens; unparseable responses score incorrect). Each condition asks one question with a manufactured upstream context, so a phase's effect is not mixed with the model's own earlier answers.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{E1, image effect} (AIA, LIL, DSCR): accuracy with the image minus text only, paired by patient, with a patient-bootstrap interval. The margin is $\pm10$ percentage points, fixed in advance; ``within margin'' requires the whole interval inside it, and the interval half-width is about $1.96\sqrt{d/n}$ for a share $d$ of items whose correctness differs, so at $n=46$ the margin is reachable only if $d\le12\%$ (10\% at $n=38$). Holm correction over the 12 model-phase tests; other contrasts are descriptive and unadjusted, with exact McNemar tests \citep{mcnemar1947note} and Wilson intervals \citep{wilson1927probable} where a proportion is reported. \textbf{AIA is the positive control:} the plan's operational reading rule is that for a model whose unadjusted AIA interval is not above zero, the test has not been shown to detect image use, and E1 on LIL and DSCR supports no conclusion about it. This operational gate is separate from confirmatory significance, which is the Holm-adjusted test. Sequence identification and lesion localization are different skills, so we also describe each phase by its own E1 and E2 results.
    \item \textbf{E2, donor tracking}: swap the image for a donor's with a different key (one donor per patient and phase, same number of images); $G=1[\text{swap}=\text{donor key}]-1[\text{text only}=\text{donor key}]$, so the no-image prior is subtracted.
    \item \textbf{E3, use of stated facts} (TCM): with the true context, move the stated timing across the 12-week window (\emph{flip\_window}) or replace the stated category by one that maps to another action class (\emph{flip\_label}). The registered summary was the share of answers equal to the rule's new answer; because that counts answers that had not changed, we report each item's transition relative to the same item under the true context (moved to the rule's new answer, already there, unchanged elsewhere, changed to another answer), and take moved over items whose original answer was not already the new one as the rate of interest. A category lookup alone reaches at most \vfourTcmLookupEsc{}\% accuracy (\vfourTcmLookupConf{}\% if it always confirms first) and cannot follow the timing flip.
    \item \textbf{E4, upstream context}: true, randomly wrong (each earlier answer replaced by a seeded random incorrect option) or absent context.
    \item \textbf{E5, PJRF} (secondary; results in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-handling}): Brier score and skill against the leave-one-out base rate, and AUC.
\end{itemize}

We report three observable outcomes separately: accuracy benefit (E1), output sensitivity (the share of items whose answer letter differs between the image and text-only arms, and between the swapped and own image), and correct donor tracking (E2). None of them observes internal processing: a model can use an image and still answer wrongly, and an unchanged answer does not show unchanged processing. Output sensitivity, the transition tables and the output-handling counts are post hoc, descriptive analyses. The ordinary five-phase chain (own answers as context) is run with and without images as a secondary analysis; each arm regenerates its upstream answers, so later-phase differences are total effects through the chain.

\paragraph{Reference model.} Three of the four open models did not meet the operational AIA gate (Section~\ref{sec:results}), so we added one reference (positive-control) model to the plan post hoc: Gemini-3.6-Flash (\texttt{gemini-3.6-flash}, Google API). It runs E1 and E2 only, with the same prompts, temperature 0 and decision rules. Two differences are forced by the API: the output cap is 16,384 tokens, because the model's hidden reasoning tokens count against it, and the route is the provider's OpenAI-compatible endpoint. The plan's Gemini-2.5-Pro was no longer offered to new accounts, and a Pro preview model was kept as the fallback should the Flash model fail the control. The control is reported apart from the four-model Holm family, empty API responses are counted and rerun (there were \vfourCtrlEmpty{} among \vfourCtrlRecords{} records), and, being an API model, it can change without notice. A second post hoc addition is a precision control: Gemma-3-27B is rerun in bf16 (Google's \texttt{gemma-3-27b-it} weights, vLLM, one GPU) on the same three conditions and phases, reported apart from the Holm family. Its reading rule was fixed in advance: an AIA interval above zero and clearly above the 4-bit model's would mean quantization explains part of the gap, and a result near chance that it does not; we do not adopt the second reading (Results). The serving stack differs from the Ollama runs, so precision and stack are confounded.

\subsection{Image controls and stated facts}

\paragraph{The reference model reads the image.} Gemini-3.6-Flash identifies the sequence for \vfourEoneOwnAIAGemini{}\% of patients with the image and \vfourEoneTextAIAGemini{}\% without it (difference \vfourEoneDiffAIAGemini{} points, 95\% CI [\vfourEoneLoAIAGemini{}, \vfourEoneHiAIAGemini{}]), and it places the lesion for \vfourEoneOwnLILGemini{}\% against \vfourEoneTextLILGemini{}\%. With the image swapped for a donor's it gives the donor's answer for \vfourEtwoSwapAIAGemini{}\% (AIA) and \vfourEtwoSwapLILGemini{}\% (LIL) of patients (Table~\ref{tab:ctrl}). A model that reads the slices can therefore answer these items, which shows the tasks are feasible; it does not show that every item is valid, and a weak result for another model on them reflects that model's performance on the task, whether from perception, input handling or training. It misses only \vfourRefErrAIA{} AIA items and \vfourRefErrLIL{} LIL items. DSCR is different. The reference model scores \vfourEoneOwnDSCRGemini{}\% with the image and \vfourEoneTextDSCRGemini{}\% without ($\Delta$ \vfourEoneDiffDSCRGemini{} [\vfourEoneLoDSCRGemini{}, \vfourEoneHiDSCRGemini{}]), where the most common key covers \vfourMajorityDSCR{}\% of items, so most of its text-only accuracy is the class prior. Its answers do depend on the image, since a swap changes \vfourEtwoChangesDSCRGemini{}\% of them (gain \vfourEtwoGainDSCRGemini{} [\vfourEtwoLoDSCRGemini{}, \vfourEtwoHiDSCRGemini{}]), but even this model recovers the key for only \vfourEoneOwnDSCRGemini{}\% of items, so the DSCR items are only partly answerable from the slices, whether from perception, from segmentation-derived keys the model does not see, or from key error, which we cannot separate. A null DSCR effect for another model therefore cannot be read as a failure to use the image.

\paragraph{Open models: image effect (E1).} Sequence identification is \vfourEoneOwnAIAGemmaTwelve{} to \vfourEoneOwnAIAScout{}\% (chance 25\%; Table~\ref{tab:e1}). One E1 test survives Holm correction, Llama-4-Scout on DSCR (\vfourEoneDiffDSCRScout{} [\vfourEoneLoDSCRScout{}, \vfourEoneHiDSCRScout{}], adjusted $p=\vfourEoneHolmDSCRScout{}$); every other adjusted $p$ is at least \vfourEoneHolmDSCRGemmaTwelve{}. That gain does not show that Scout reads the category from the slices: with the image its accuracy (\vfourEoneOwnDSCRScout{}\%) stays below the share of the most common key (\vfourMajorityDSCR{}\%), and the gain arises because without the image it falls to \vfourEoneTextDSCRScout{}\%, far below that share. The plan's operational AIA gate (unadjusted interval above zero) is met only by Llama-4-Scout (\vfourEoneDiffAIAScout{} [\vfourEoneLoAIAScout{}, \vfourEoneHiAIAScout{}]), whose gain is small beside the reference model's, and not by MedGemma-4B (\vfourEoneDiffAIAMedGemma{} [\vfourEoneLoAIAMedGemma{}, \vfourEoneHiAIAMedGemma{}]), Gemma-3-12B (\vfourEoneDiffAIAGemmaTwelve{} [\vfourEoneLoAIAGemmaTwelve{}, \vfourEoneHiAIAGemmaTwelve{}]) or Gemma-3-27B (\vfourEoneDiffAIAGemmaTwentySeven{} [\vfourEoneLoAIAGemmaTwentySeven{}, \vfourEoneHiAIAGemmaTwentySeven{}]); the gate depends on interval width. For the three models below the gate, the plan's reading rule permits no interpretation of their LIL or DSCR effects. On LIL every interval includes zero, with point estimates from \vfourEoneDiffLILMedGemma{} to \vfourEoneDiffLILGemmaTwelve{}; on DSCR Gemma-3-27B is within the margin (\vfourEoneDiffDSCRGemmaTwentySeven{}), while MedGemma-4B (\vfourEoneDiffDSCRMedGemma{} [\vfourEoneLoDSCRMedGemma{}, \vfourEoneHiDSCRMedGemma{}]) and Gemma-3-12B (\vfourEoneDiffDSCRGemmaTwelve{} [\vfourEoneLoDSCRGemmaTwelve{}, \vfourEoneHiDSCRGemmaTwelve{}]) have unadjusted intervals above zero (adjusted $p=\vfourEoneHolmDSCRMedGemma{}$ and $\vfourEoneHolmDSCRGemmaTwelve{}$) that the reading rule does not let us interpret; MedGemma-4B's accuracy with the image (\vfourEoneOwnDSCRMedGemma{}\%) is below chance and Gemma-3-12B's (\vfourEoneOwnDSCRGemmaTwelve{}\%) equals the most common key's share. Each open model's AIA answers also concentrate on one sequence (its most frequent answer covers \vfourAiaModalShareLo{} to \vfourAiaModalShareHi{}\% of the \vfourNAIA{} items), which is what a fixed default plus weak image use would look like; we cannot separate that from a failure to perceive the sequence, although every image does reach the model in each pipeline (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-handling}). Accuracy benefit is small, but answers are not insensitive to the image: the answer differs between the image and text-only arms for \vfourSensRangeOwnTextAIALo{} to \vfourSensRangeOwnTextAIAHi{}\% of AIA items, \vfourSensRangeOwnTextLILLo{} to \vfourSensRangeOwnTextLILHi{}\% of LIL items and \vfourSensRangeOwnTextDSCRLo{} to \vfourSensRangeOwnTextDSCRHi{}\% of DSCR items across the four models (Table~\ref{tab:e1}).

\paragraph{Precision control.} Rerun in bf16, Gemma-3-27B identifies the sequence for \vfourEoneOwnAIAGemmaTwentySevenBf{}\% of patients with the image and \vfourEoneTextAIAGemmaTwentySevenBf{}\% without it ($\Delta$ \vfourEoneDiffAIAGemmaTwentySevenBf{} [\vfourEoneLoAIAGemmaTwentySevenBf{}, \vfourEoneHiAIAGemmaTwentySevenBf{}]), against \vfourEoneOwnAIAGemmaTwentySeven{}\% and \vfourEoneTextAIAGemmaTwentySeven{}\% at 4-bit and \vfourEoneOwnAIAGemini{}\% for the reference model. The interval includes zero and the accuracy stays near chance (25\%), so the rerun did not restore AIA performance, and this experiment gives no evidence that changing precision and serving stack resolves the gap. Precision and stack changed together, and a null result cannot show that quantization has no effect, so we draw no conclusion about quantization. On LIL and DSCR the bf16 model is also indistinguishable from text-only (\vfourEoneDiffLILGemmaTwentySevenBf{} and \vfourEoneDiffDSCRGemmaTwentySevenBf{}), and donor tracking on AIA is \vfourEtwoGainAIAGemmaTwentySevenBf{} [\vfourEtwoLoAIAGemmaTwentySevenBf{}, \vfourEtwoHiAIAGemmaTwentySevenBf{}]. Only one model was rerun.

\paragraph{Open models: donor tracking (E2).} Swapping the image changes the answer for \vfourSensRangeSwapAIALo{} to \vfourSensRangeSwapAIAHi{}\% of AIA items and \vfourSensRangeSwapLILLo{} to \vfourSensRangeSwapLILHi{}\% of LIL items, but the new answer follows the donor's key with an interval above zero in only two cells (Table~\ref{tab:e2}): MedGemma-4B on AIA (gain \vfourEtwoGainAIAMedGemma{} [\vfourEtwoLoAIAMedGemma{}, \vfourEtwoHiAIAMedGemma{}]) and Llama-4-Scout on DSCR (\vfourEtwoGainDSCRScout{} [\vfourEtwoLoDSCRScout{}, \vfourEtwoHiDSCRScout{}]; a swap changes \vfourEtwoChangesDSCRScout{}\% of its DSCR answers), the phase in which its accuracy gain also survives correction. A swap changes \vfourEtwoChangesDSCRGemmaTwentySeven{}\% of Gemma-3-27B's DSCR answers. \paragraph{Chain (secondary).} With the model's own answers as context, most image-minus-text differences are small and their intervals include zero. The exception is Llama-4-Scout on AIA (\vfourEsixDiffAIAScout{} [\vfourEsixLoAIAScout{}, \vfourEsixHiAIAScout{}]), which repeats its E1 result. Its DSCR difference (\vfourEsixDiffDSCRScout{} [\vfourEsixLoDSCRScout{}, \vfourEsixHiDSCRScout{}]) has a lower limit at zero and is much smaller than its E1 gain; its text-only arm scores higher in the chain than in E1, and we did not test why. The largest other difference is Gemma-3-12B on LIL (\vfourEsixDiffLILGemmaTwelve{} [\vfourEsixLoLILGemmaTwelve{}, \vfourEsixHiLILGemmaTwelve{}]). These are not corrected for multiplicity.

\paragraph{What the chain adds.} Independent questions cannot show whether a model uses its own earlier answers or the stated diagnosis in a later step; a chain can. Here that matters for one phase only: true upstream context beats no context for TCM in MedGemma-4B, Gemma-3-12B and Gemma-3-27B (unadjusted), and the fact flips show which stated facts move the TCM answer (Tables~\ref{tab:e3} and~\ref{tab:e3trans}), whereas for LIL and DSCR the true context does not help in any model and the ordinary chain gives small total effects. The clearest findings of this paper therefore concern the reference model's single AIA and LIL questions and their donor swaps, and Llama-4-Scout's DSCR answers, and the chain's added value is limited to TCM's dependence on context and stated facts; we do not claim a causal-degradation effect across phases.

\paragraph{What this shows and does not show.} We separate three observable outcomes. \emph{Accuracy benefit:} the reference model gains \vfourEoneDiffAIAGemini{} and \vfourEoneDiffLILGemini{} points from the image on sequence and lesion side, whereas the open models gain \vfourEoneDiffAIAGemmaTwelve{} to \vfourEoneDiffAIAScout{} points on sequence with an interval that excludes zero only for Llama-4-Scout, not surviving Holm correction; the one open-model result that survives it, Llama-4-Scout on DSCR, stays below the most common key's share. \emph{Output sensitivity:} answers do change with the image in all four open models, so the image is not simply ignored (Table~\ref{tab:e1}). \emph{Donor tracking:} swapped-image answers move toward the donor's key with an interval above zero only for MedGemma-4B on sequence (\vfourEtwoGainAIAMedGemma{} points) and Llama-4-Scout on the diagnosis category (\vfourEtwoGainDSCRScout{}), against \vfourEtwoGainAIAGemini{} and \vfourEtwoGainLILGemini{} for the reference model on sequence and lesion side. The evaluated open models therefore show limited and inconsistent image-dependent performance on these tasks, and the reference model shows strong image dependence on items it can answer. This does not identify a mechanism: a model can use an image and still answer wrongly, and unchanged answers do not show unchanged processing, so we make no claim of non-use, and none of learned shortcuts; the open models are smaller and differ in training from the reference model, either of which would produce the same gap. On the management rule no model exceeds the best category lookup in accuracy, stating the rule in the prompt raises accuracy for three of the four, and their sensitivity to the stated category and timing differs by model (Tables~\ref{tab:e3} and~\ref{tab:e3trans}). For DSCR the most common key covers \vfourMajorityDSCR{}\% of items, and Gemma-3-27B (both arms) and Gemma-3-12B (with the image) score exactly that, so accuracy on DSCR alone cannot separate image use from that prior, and we draw no conclusion about image use from it. Input handling was checked only in part (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-handling} and Limitations). None of this is validated by clinicians.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Accuracy on an expert-labeled brain-MRI chain cannot show whether models read the scan when the labels are not validated targets for what is displayed: an automated rule reproduces the expert RANO rating for \vfourConcordantPct{}\% of follow-ups (\vfourConcordantDetPct{}\% of determinate ones), a discrepancy we cannot yet attribute, and the benchmark we started from did not, under its released identifiers and our matching, establish same-patient chains. We build the chain on LUMIERE with a stated origin for each key and fixed a control suite in advance, with a positive control that only the image can answer, matched donor swaps, fixed-context comparisons and fact-flip tests of a management rule. A reference model reads the sequence and the lesion side from the image (\vfourEoneOwnAIAGemini{}\% and \vfourEoneOwnLILGemini{}\%, against \vfourEoneTextAIAGemini{}\% and \vfourEoneTextLILGemini{}\% without it). Four open-weight models show limited and inconsistent image-dependent performance: small accuracy gains, of which only Llama-4-Scout's on the diagnosis category survives multiplicity correction (and there stays below the most common key's share), answers that change with the image, donor tracking whose interval excludes zero only for MedGemma-4B on sequence and Llama-4-Scout on the diagnosis category, and no result above the best category lookup on the management rule. These results do not identify whether the models use the image internally, or why, and model scale, training, serving stack and 4-bit serving are not separated from the gap. Clinician review of the keys, in particular of the segmentation-derived targets, the management rule and the assumed radiotherapy schedule, is the outstanding step, and only one proprietary reference model is run. The controls apply to any chain-structured medical benchmark whose keys have a stated origin that can be checked against its inputs.



\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Clinician review is outstanding.} No clinician has judged whether each question is answerable from the inputs shown or whether each key is right. The LIL and DSCR keys are computed from automated segmentation masks (DeepBraTumIA) that the model does not see, so they are targets that a reader can recover from the slices only if the segmentation is right, and segmentation errors propagate to them. The reference model's results support the feasibility of the AIA and LIL tasks, not the validity of every item. The enhancement rule ignores T2/FLAIR change, clinical status and steroid dose, abstains where no measurable enhancing disease exists, and reproduces the expert category for \vfourConcordantPct{}\% of follow-ups (\vfourConcordantDetPct{}\% of determinate ones); disagreement can come from segmentation or measurement error, from a different target lesion or reference scan, from the simplified rule, or from evidence outside the displayed slices, and the audit does not separate these. Consequently our complete response label is weaker than clinical RANO's: it is assigned whenever follow-up enhancement falls below the measurability threshold, which can include residual sub-threshold enhancing disease, not clinical RANO's sustained disappearance of all enhancing and nonmeasurable disease. The rule also tracks a single lesion (the enhancing component nearest the baseline target lesion's location) and has no RANO new-lesion criterion, under which an unequivocal new lesion is itself progressive disease; of the \vfourNewLesionN{} follow-ups where the rule says complete response but the expert rated progressive disease, \vfourNewLesionPlausible{} show a distinct, newly measurable enhancing component elsewhere consistent with a missed new lesion, \vfourNewLesionPreexisting{} show a similarly sized component already present at the nadir, and \vfourNewLesionNone{} show no other measurable enhancing component at all, so this disagreement is not entirely explained by the tracking rule. The expert rating is recorded but is not the key.

\textbf{The management key is a rule, not a documented treatment.} LUMIERE records no treatment after chemoradiotherapy. The TCM key rests on an assumed radiotherapy schedule (ending 10 weeks after surgery) and on a simplified reading of the 12-week RANO window, which also allows progression inside the window when new enhancement lies outside the radiation field. The PJRF outcome assumes that survival time and scan weeks share the surgery-week origin, and censoring is not documented. With \vfourPjrfN{} patients a leave-one-out logistic model on the stated facts did not improve on the base rate (Brier \vfourPjrfBrierLR{} against \vfourPjrfBrierBase{}), which does not show that the facts carry no predictive information; the base rate reflects our stratified selection, and because the outcome construction is unverified PJRF is secondary and supports no claim here.

\textbf{Power and scope.} With \vfourNLIL{} to \vfourNAIA{} patients per phase, the pre-specified $\pm10$-point margin is reachable only when few items differ between arms, and several cells will be inconclusive. Only one E1 image-effect test (Llama-4-Scout on DSCR) survives Holm correction, and three of the four open models (MedGemma-4B, Gemma-3-12B, Gemma-3-27B) fail the plan's own AIA positive-control gate, so under the preregistered rule their LIL and DSCR effects support no conclusion at all, not merely a weak one. The positive control tests one skill, sequence identification; passing it does not show that a model can read lesions. The donor images come from different patients, so anatomy differs beyond the tested feature; stems in the text-only arm still refer to images. Patients are from one center and the cohort was stratified by design; the four open-weight models run at 4-bit through Ollama while the control is an API model of unknown size and precision, so the gap between them mixes scale, quantization and training and cannot be attributed to shortcut learning; a bf16 rerun of Gemma-3-27B (through vLLM, so precision is confounded with the serving stack) stays near chance on AIA, which does not resolve the gap but cannot isolate quantization from the serving stack, and no other model was rerun. There is one reference model, run once at temperature 0, and API models change without notice. \textbf{Input handling was checked only in part.} We confirmed post hoc, for each pipeline, that every image of a question raises the prompt token count (including the second and third DSCR images) and that known-answer probes are answered correctly (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-handling}). We did not compare the images each serving stack passes to its encoder with a reference implementation, and a check on one patient cannot show that MRI contrast is preserved by each model's image encoder. Near-chance sequence identification in several open models therefore remains unexplained: the check makes a dropped-image bug unlikely but does not exclude weak encoder features or default-answer behavior. MedGemma-4B returned invalid JSON for \vfourParseInvalidMedGemma{} of \vfourParseNMedGemma{} records, and Llama-4-Scout for \vfourParseInvalidScout{} of \vfourParseNScout{}; the answer letter was recovered by pattern for all but \vfourParseNoLetterMedGemma{} records (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-handling}). The comparison to the reference model mixes scale, training, quantization and serving. Intervals treat patients as exchangeable.

\section{OmniBrainBench Compatibility Details}
\label{sec:app-compat}

A chain presupposes that a case's phases are one patient's trajectory. We tested this on OmniBrainBench \citep{peng2026omnibrainbench} by checking whether any image is shared across questions of different phases within a grouped case. In the 6,823-question closed-ended subset (15 source sub-corpora) only \textbf{2} verifiable cross-phase patient links exist, both in VQA\_RAD (one AIA and one LIL question each); a second method, stripping modality, view and slice suffixes from filenames, gave the same result. The release has no complete patient identifier, so these are lower bounds. The cause is structural: each sub-corpus was built for one clinical task, so a patient is essentially asked one phase-type of question, and reading \texttt{source\_file} as patient identity was our choice that stitches together different patients. This is an incompatibility with our evaluation, not a defect in a promise its authors made. The supplement's patient-level ADNI extension is not a brain-tumor cohort and, as of September 2026, has label-generation scripts only; we did not evaluate it. We found no replacement dataset with same-patient, multi-phase continuity and a question layer (Section~\ref{sec:related-work}), which motivates the LUMIERE construction.

MedGemma-4B's DSCR accuracy on valid-JSON records (\vfourDscrAccValidMedGemma{}\%) is close to all records (\vfourDscrAccAllMedGemma{}\%), so parsing does not explain its below-chance score, which comes from the letters it chooses (never the fourth option, in either arm). These counts alone do not show that images reach each model correctly; the input check below does.

\paragraph{Input check.} For each pipeline we sent one real AIA, one LIL and one three-image DSCR item with zero to three of the item's images, behind a unique nonce (prevents server-side prefix caching), and read the prompt token count. Each added image raises the count by a similar amount (Table~\ref{tab:inputcheck}), so no image was dropped, including the second and third DSCR images. The same pipelines answered known-answer probes in the same message format (solid colours, printed numbers, image order and count, a real slice's black background), and one slice remained legible squashed to 896 and 448 pixels. These checks show images reach the model and simple stimuli are perceived; run once, on one patient, they do not show that MRI-specific contrast survives each model's image encoder.
